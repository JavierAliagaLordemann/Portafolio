1: Between Difference and Map: Power and Limits of the Rhizome as a Way of Thinking

By: Javier Aliaga Lordemann

Gilles Deleuze's philosophy begins with a radical assertion: difference precedes identity. There is no stable essence that later diversifies; reality is, from the outset, multiplicity in becoming. This ontological inversion breaks with the representational tradition that orders the world through fixed categories and stable hierarchies. Thinking from difference means accepting that reality is not exhausted by classifications, but continuously overflows any attempt at closure.

Repetition, in this framework, is not simple reiteration of the same. It is the mechanism through which difference actualizes itself and produces novelty. Each repetition contains variation, displacement, transformation. However, this thesis faces an obvious counterexample: there are practices that repeat without innovating, systems that reproduce structures almost intact over long periods. This forces us to recognize that repetition is not creative by definition, but only under specific conditions.

The notion of multiplicity reinforces this dynamic vision. Entities are not homogeneous blocks, but assemblages of forces, relations, and flows. An institution, a subject, or a social phenomenon are always partial and changing compositions. Nevertheless, asserting that everything is multiplicity can lead to excessive generalization. If all structure is fluid and all identity is provisional, the difficulty arises of explaining the relative stability that also characterizes many systems.

The concept of the rhizome, developed with Félix Guattari, is perhaps the most influential image of this ontology. Against the arboreal model—with a single root and vertical hierarchy—the rhizome proposes an underground network of multiple connections, without a fixed center or predetermined order. Any point can connect with any other. This image questions centralization and opens the possibility of thinking decentralized and dynamic structures.

However, the sharp opposition between rhizome and hierarchy can become a false dichotomy. There are highly efficient hierarchical organizations that do not collapse due to their verticality. Similarly, horizontal networks can become chaotic or inefficient. Deleuzian critique is powerful as a corrective against excessive rigidity, but does not constitute an absolute negation of all hierarchical form.

Another delicate point is the critique of teleology. Deleuze distrusts transcendent ends that orient history toward predetermined goals. But in practice, many systems operate with clear and measurable objectives. The total absence of purpose can hinder decision-making. The tension between openness to becoming and the need for strategic direction remains without definitive resolution.

From a logical standpoint, Deleuzian thought faces additional challenges. Conceptual ambiguity can facilitate multiple interpretations, but also weakens argumentative precision. The aesthetic appeal to metaphors like the rhizome can substitute for rigorous demonstrations. There is a risk of circularity when difference is always explained by more difference, without external criteria for contrast.

Similarly, the difficulty of falsification limits its empirical scope. If every phenomenon can be reinterpreted as assemblage or becoming, it is difficult to establish conditions under which the theory could be considered refuted. This does not invalidate its critical power, but obliges us to recognize its more heuristic than predictive character.

Despite these limitations, the Deleuzian proposal offers an extraordinary conceptual tool for rethinking complex processes. The idea of assemblage allows us to understand how heterogeneous elements articulate themselves provisionally without losing their singularity. The actualization of the virtual describes how latent potentialities become concrete under certain historical conditions.

When the rhizome is transferred from the ontological plane to the methodological, it becomes a way of exploring without hierarchizing in advance. Instead of starting from rigid categories, open starting points are identified—problems or themes that are not subordinated to a central axis. What matters is not establishing an initial order, but allowing the proliferation of connections.

The second movement consists of tracing multiple links, even unexpected ones. Richness emerges from the intersection between dimensions that are traditionally studied separately. Connections can be strong or weak, visible or hidden, direct or mediated. The resulting map is not linear, but reticular.

Here cartography appears as an analytical layer. It is not just another node, but an exercise in reading the network. It consists of identifying densities, detecting zones of high connection and dispersed areas, recognizing emergent patterns. Cartography does not fix the network; it interprets it provisionally.

Emergent lines are not defined in advance. They arise where connections intensify and reveal possible trajectories. Not all denser connections should become priority axes, but they do indicate potentialities that deserve attention. Emergence replaces rigid planning.

Ruptures play a fundamental role. They are absent connections that should exist, voids that reveal structural gaps. Detecting them allows us to problematize what is given and open new possibilities. Absence thus becomes a critical indicator.

This methodology assumes the constant possibility of reconfiguration. Networks can expand, contract, or transform according to new evidence or actors. There is no definitive closure. Each cartography is provisional and susceptible to revision.

Nevertheless, permanent openness can generate dispersion. Without criteria for prioritization or moments of synthesis, the process risks dissolving. Creativity needs points of condensation to not become infinite indeterminacy.

Therefore, a rigorous application of the rhizome requires combining open exploration with instances of strategic articulation. The network does not eliminate the need for decision; it enriches it with greater awareness of interdependencies. Structure does not disappear, but becomes more reflexive.

Ultimately, the value of the rhizome as methodology lies in its capacity to reveal complexities invisible under linear models. Its limit appears when it is absolutized and becomes an anti-structural dogma. The critical power of difference should not be confused with the negation of all form of order.

To make this clear, imagine a group of neighbors wanting to improve their neighborhood. Instead of starting with a rigid plan where someone defines "the main problem" and establishes an ordered list of priorities, they decide to meet and simply put all their concerns on the table: security, garbage, green areas, transportation, cultural activities, lighting, youth employment. They do not attempt to hierarchize anything at the start. Each topic is just a starting point.

Then they begin to converse and connect ideas. Someone says that more lighting can improve security. Another person comments that organizing cultural activities reduces vandalism because young people become more involved. Someone else relates green areas to greater coexistence and well-being, which can also diminish conflicts. Little by little, topics that seemed separate begin to intertwine.

In that process, some connections become stronger than others. The group notices that lighting, community activities, and security appear constantly linked. That network of connections begins to outline a possible line of action: strengthen community life and improve public space as an integral security strategy. That idea was not defined from the start; it emerged from the crossing of multiple relations.

At the same time, they realize that almost no one mentioned the transportation problem, although it is evident that it affects many. That absence reveals a rupture, a gap in the conversation. Detecting it allows opening a new line of discussion and expanding the map of relations.

Finally, the group observes the set of connections: where there are many relations, where there are few, what topics concentrate more energy, and which have remained isolated. That reading of the map helps them decide where to start. Thus, they first freely explore the connections and then, with greater understanding of the whole, prioritize concrete actions.

Thinking with Deleuze means accepting the tension between openness and form, between becoming and organization. The rhizome is not a universal recipe, but an invitation to map without closing, to connect without reducing, and to recognize that all structure is provisional in the face of the dynamism of reality.
