Overpricing? Or Trader Premiums - By Javier Aliaga Lordemann - An analysis that examines margins in trading, debating whether they are overpricing or fair compensation for the risk and expertise of traders.

1: Overpricing? Or Trader Premiums

By: Javier Aliaga Lordemann

In recent days, the idea has taken hold that Bolivia would be paying an "overpricing" of around USD 4 million for the purchase of oil. The figure arises from comparing two different contracts for delivery in Sica Sica and Pocitos. In the case of Pocitos, which is more striking, there is a contract with a trader "premium" of 9.10 USD per barrel and another where the premium rises to 20.88 USD per barrel. The difference is large: 11.78 dollars per barrel. But does that mean overpricing or could there be a technical explanation?

The final price at the border is not just the international value of crude oil, it includes the "premium" which is the additional margin that the trader charges above the international base price (PPI). It includes their profit and, depending on the contract, can cover costs such as financing, commercial risk, logistics management or product availability. It is calculated as follows:

Final price (USD/barrel) = PPI + trader premium + transport + insurance + taxes.

The PPI is set in the transitional period at 64.45 USD per barrel according to current regulations. That is the starting point; if the PPI were greater than 64.45 USD from the start, one could assert that there is overpricing. If we apply the formula to the contract with a premium of 9.10 USD, the illustrative calculation would be: 64.45 + 9.10 + transport + insurance. If we assume 3 USD for transport and 0.30 for insurance, the final price would be approximately 76.85 USD per barrel. Now let's do the same with the premium of 20.88 USD: 64.45 + 20.88 + 3 + 0.30 = 88.63 USD per barrel. The final difference matches exactly the jump in the premium: 11.78 USD per barrel. Multiplied by the volume — that difference explains the almost 4 million dollars. That is, the arithmetic checks out, the increase exists in simple numbers. The relevant question is whether the comparison is technically valid.

This is where the most important part comes in. Two contracts can have very different premiums for legitimate reasons, as we said: different quality of crude, different delivery conditions (who pays the freight), market timing, urgency in the purchase or financial payment conditions (deferred payments, etc.). It is not the same as an internal operation – Botrading - or related as a purchase from an international trader under supply pressure. Each additional risk assumed by the supplier is reflected in the premium. If the premium rises (from 9 to 20), the market is charging more to assume the risk of selling to YPFB. The key is not that it rises, but why it rises and whether it is justified; and what that says about YPFB's strength as a company.

That is why asserting "overpricing" requires reviewing the complete contracts: transport clauses, insurance included, technical specification of the crude, payment terms and actual invoices.
