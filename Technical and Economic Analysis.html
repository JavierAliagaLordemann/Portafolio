1: Technical-Economic Analysis of DS 5549: Distributed Generation and Subsidized Electricity Pricing in Bolivia

By: Javier Aliaga Lordemann

The new Supreme Decree 5549 has arrived to change the rules of the electrical game in Bolivia. It is not just another piece of paper; it is the recognition that the "cheap gas" model is running out. For the first time, the door opens for industries and commerce to install their own solar plants of up to 6 MW and sell their surplus to the grid. But is it really a good business?

In essence, this decree bets on Distributed Generation. Imagine moving from depending on a single giant and distant plant to having thousands of small plants on the roofs of our factories. This converts the company into a "prosumer": someone who produces what they consume and helps the State not have to burn as much gas to give us light.

However, there is an invisible wall: the subsidy. Today, electricity in Bolivia maintains an appearance of low cost because natural gas for state thermoelectric plants is delivered at minimal prices. This illusion has a massive real cost: while the bill seems comfortable, the country sacrifices millions of dollars it would obtain by exporting that gas or by stopping diesel imports. The sun is a free source, but the technology to harness it must compete against a state input artificially made cheap; that is the first major profitability challenge.

Here enters the "Cost War." Installing solar panels requires dollars, and we all know that today the dollar has a real price much higher than the official one. This makes setting up a solar plant more expensive than it should be. If the Government wants private parties to invest, it must pay them for their clean energy at a price that recognizes the foreign exchange savings they are generating for the country.

Another obstacle is the paradox of overcapacity. Bolivia has infrastructure to produce double the electricity it consumes. The problem is that this infrastructure depends on gas that is already scarce. We have "lots of iron," but little fuel. If the regulation does not guarantee that private solar energy is used with priority, investments will remain stalled while the State continues burning gas out of pure technical inertia.

That is why the success of DS 5549 depends on solar energy being "Bankable." No bank will lend money for a 5 million dollar plant if there is no secure contract guaranteeing that the grid will buy that energy. We need long-term contracts and clear rules ensuring that, if there is sun, that energy is used first, displacing old and wasteful thermoelectric plants.

We cannot ignore that the electricity rate in Bolivia is a "pressure cooker." With high real inflation and lack of dollars, keeping the lights cheap is increasingly difficult for the State. The incentive for an industry to install solar panels will be born, unfortunately, from the "pain" of the next tariff adjustments. When the bill goes up, solar energy will stop being an ecological option to become a survival strategy.

For this decree not to die on a desk, the regulation must be bold. We propose three pillars: a fair Incentive Rate that rewards gas savings, mechanisms for investment recovery through tax discounts (tax compensation) and contracts that give total legal security to the investor against distributors.

The true energy transition in Bolivia is not a romantic topic about the environment; it is a matter of economic survival. Each installed solar panel is gas the country saves and dollars the Central Bank stops spending on imports. The private sector is ready to invest, but it needs the terrain to stop being swampy and become a solid foundation of trust.

In conclusion, DS 5549 is the blueprint of a modern building, but today it is designed on the mud of subsidies. If we manage to make prices transparent and value real savings, Bolivia could experience an unprecedented energy revolution. Time is running out: in the next 24 months, energy self-sufficiency will go from being a wish to a critical necessity for national industry.
